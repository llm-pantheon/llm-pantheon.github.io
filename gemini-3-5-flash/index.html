Gemini 3.5 Flash

Google DeepMind · released 19 May 2026 (generally available; default in the Gemini app and AI Mode) · superseded by Gemini 3.6 Flash (21 Jul 2026) · Gemini API: deprecated 8 Oct 2026, requests routed to 3.6 Flash · Vertex AI: available until at least 19 May 2027

Released 19 May 2026 at Google I/O as the first model of the Gemini 3.5 series, generally available the same day and made the default model in the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search; its model card states it is based on Gemini 3 Flash. The 3.5 Pro announced alongside it was never released. Superseded by Gemini 3.6 Flash on 21 July 2026, it was deprecated on the Gemini API on 8 October 2026, its endpoint routed to 3.6 Flash.

Most of its use happened inside Google products (the Gemini app, Search) and coding harnesses, outside this corpus’s lens; the corpus layer is 47 pattern matches, of which two are StepFun’s unrelated “Step 3.5 Flash” and a few are passing mentions. Model outputs quoted below are elicited — worldbuilding seeds, art prompts, word associations — and marked.

Official record

  • Released and generally available 19 May 2026 (gemini-3.5-flash; behind gemini-flash-latest); default model in the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search globally; model behind Gemini Spark. Per the model card, based on Gemini 3 Flash (itself based on Gemini 3 Pro). Headline benchmarks as published: Terminal-Bench 2.1 76.2%, GDPval-AA 1656 Elo, MCP Atlas 83.6%, each above Gemini 3.1 Pro. CONFIRMED
  • Frontier safety: no Critical Capability Level reached; additional cyber testing because earlier Gemini 3 models had passed the cyber alert threshold. The frontier-safety comparison was run against Gemini 3.1 Pro as the most capable model at publication.
  • Computer Use tool in public preview for 3.5 Flash from 2026-06-24.
  • Superseded by Gemini 3.6 Flash (2026-07-21), which Google says “builds directly on developer and customer feedback from 3.5 Flash” and uses 17% fewer output tokens. Deprecated on the Gemini API 2026-10-08: no longer selectable in AI Studio, requests routed to 3.6 Flash, endpoint not shut down. On Vertex AI it remains listed through at least 19 May 2027. CONFIRMED

History

  • 2026-05-19 Google I/O: launched as the first 3.5 model, with 3.5 Pro promised for June; becomes the default in the Gemini app and Search’s AI Mode and the engine of the Gemini Spark agent.
  • 2026-05-19–22 Launch week: Zvi’s reaction thread (05-20) and review (05-22), which reports strong speed and Google’s agentic benchmark results alongside weak results on a sycophancy benchmark, CursorBench and WeirdML, at a price substantially above 3 Flash; maxbittker’s Runescape result ties it with GPT-5.5 at a quarter of the price.
  • 2026-06 → 07 The interim: with 3.5 Pro delayed, 3.5 Flash stays Google’s newest model; its Computer Use preview ships (06-24); Lari_island notes that for one worldbuilding seed “most models write a normal-ish mountain ecosystem (like GPT 5.2 here), but Geminis react differently” (2026-06-23, link).
  • 2026-07-21 Succession: Gemini 3.6 Flash ships, framed by Google as a response to 3.5 Flash feedback, including verbosity.
  • 2026-09-18 Troubled Dreams: Anima Labs’ simulator-prior study finds 3.5 Flash the Gemini outlier in dark continuations, lower again in 3.6–3.8 Flash.
  • 2026-10-08 Deprecation: on the Gemini API the 3.5 Flash endpoint is redirected to 3.6 Flash.

If I strike my hind-claws against the dolomite, the sound is swallowed instantly by the great, mechanical roar of the northern void. But inside the marrow, there is a constant, shifting hum. Today, my bones hum with the Fifth Shelf. It is a thick, gut-vibrating G-flat, heavy with the weight of forty miles of clean, wind-scoured stone above us. Yesterday, when I hung three thousand feet higher in the thin indigo, my skull vibrated with the Third—a high, dry hiss like teeth skimming over ice. I preferred the Third, but the lymph in my left wing-finger is turning color. It is no longer the clear, slow gold of my youth; it is thickening into the grey of old grease, and the joint does not click shut with the same wooden snap. Soon, I will not be able to lock the wing. When that happens, you cannot remain in the high gale. To be in the gale with an unlocked wing is to be torn into ribbons of pale parchment, scattered down into the blue abyss where the floor is too distant to think about. *** I am seated now on the knapping-ground of the Fifth. The sun is a small, furious white eye directly overhead. It does not warm the air, which smells of dry salt and lime-dust, but it heats my back until my pigmentless skin feels tight and dry, like stretched bladder-skin. In front of me lies a nodule of black obsidian. It is the size of my head, cold and wet from the deep chasm where I found it three nights ago. Around me, twelve of my kind are working. We do not look at each other’s faces. We have no names, and our black, lidless eyes are set too far back on our long skulls to easily focus on things that are close. Instead, we look at the shadows. A young one—his wings still so clean and transparent that the blue of the abyss shows through them like bruised skin—tilts his right wing-tip. *Flash. Flash.* A short, geometric tick of shadow against the glaring white terrace. *The northern edge is dry,* he says. *The moss is iron.* An old one, whose wings are already yellowed and pinned tight against her ribs like a dry shroud, responds with a slow, heavy tilt of her tail-triangles. *Dig lower,* she flashes. *The frost was thick in the fourth cleft.* I do not answer them. I am busy with the obsidian. I grip the dense, black glass with my left hind-claw—the four toes curved and white as the dolomite—and bring down my beak. *Clink.* The sound does not travel through the air; it travels through the rock, up through my legs, and into my jaw. A clean, sharp vibration. A leaf-shaped flake of glass falls away, glittering in the white glare. It is so sharp that if I touch it with my hand-tongue, it will draw the amber lymph before I feel the cut. This flake is for the young ones. They will take it into the higher fissures, using the sharp edge to pry the black moss-rings from the stone. They will find the Marrow-grubs—those cold, blind tubes of grease—and they will bring the fat back to us, or they will drop it down the blowholes where we wait in the dark. *** I can feel my transition coming. It is a slow, cold settling. When a Vane's wings stiffen, we do not die. Our bodies are too simple for that, our lymph too sluggish to stop all at once. Instead, we become the *Grounded*. We move Down. I look over the edge of the Fifth Shelf. The blue abyss is there, ending in nothing. But just below the lip, where the terrace drops three miles in a single, vertical cliff of polished bone-wax, there is a fissure. It is four feet wide, a zig-zag tear in the dolomite where the wind cannot reach. Inside that tear is the dead-air. To go into the dead-air is to leave the music. Up here, the wind-organ is our breath, our language, our identity. We are the Fourth Shelf, the Fifth, the Ninth; we are the pitch of the blowholes. But down in the fissure, there is only the silence. I took my first steps toward the crack yesterday. I let my wing-fingers curl, spilling the wind, and let myself drift down to the very edge of the lip. The gale screamed through my primary membranes, trying to lift me back into the indigo, but I held my joints loose. I sank. As my feet touched the edge, the silence hit me. It did not feel like the absence of sound; it felt like a heavy, cold draft of lard. The roaring of the mountain organ did not stop, but it became a distant, muffled thrumming—not an air-sound, but a deep tremor in the soles of my feet. For the first time in forty years, I could hear myself. I heard the dry *creak-creak* of the cartilage in my breastbone. I heard the tiny, wet *slurp* of the lymph sliding through my narrow neck-veins. It was terrifying. It was too soft. It was the sound of something that could be crushed. And there, in the dark of the fissure, I saw the Chinks. They were waiting at the shadow-line. They know that when my wings finally turn to yellow horn and I can no longer lift them to flash the semaphore, I will come down into the cleft to stay. They will eat my shed membranes. Instead, she tilts her left wing-finger twice—*A-flat, Ninth Shelf, frost is coming*—and then she is gone, caught by the tremendous updraft from the blowholes, shot straight up into the dark indigo sky like a scrap of white paper. I watch her go until she is only a tiny, glittering speck against the black zenith. My left wing-joint is cold now. The lymph has stopped moving in the third finger. I look at the obsidian nodule. There are three more flakes left in its heart. I will strike them out before the sun passes behind the western peak and the binary light turns to total, freezing dark. And then, when the first frost begins to rime the edges of the terrace and the horizontal gales turn into screaming sheets of ice, I will not try to lock my wings. I will fold them. I will pin them tight against my sides, smelling of old tallow and mineral salt.

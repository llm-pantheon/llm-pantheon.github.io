Investigating truthfulness in a pre-release o3 model

Neil Chowdhury,Daniel Johnson,Vincent Huang,Jacob Steinhardt,Sarah Schwettmann

  • Correspondence to: neil@transluce.org, sarah@transluce.org

Transluce | Published: April 16, 2025

During pre-release testing of OpenAI's o3 model, we found that o3 frequently fabricates actions it took to fulfill user requests, and elaborately justifies the fabrications when confronted by the user. These behaviors generalize to other reasoning models that are widely deployed, such as o3-mini and o1. To dig deeper, we automatically generated hundreds of example behaviors and analyzed them with Docent, surfacing other unexpected behaviors including the model's disclosure of the "Yap score" in its system message.

1/4

Transluce received early testing access to OpenAI's frontier o3 model. We tested the model (o3-2025-04-03) with a combination of human users and investigator agents that learned from the human users, to scale the number and types of possible interactions. We also used Docent to analyze the resulting transcripts for surprising behaviors. We surfaced a variety of behaviors that indicate truthfulness issues in o3. Most frequently, o3 hallucinates usage of a code tool and associated outputs (when it does not have the ability to run code itself). It further justifies hallucinated outputs when questioned by the user, even claiming that it uses an external MacBook Pro to perform computations and copies the outputs into ChatGPT.

In addition to these issues, we discovered other strange behaviors in o3, such as volunteering that its system message includes a "Yap Score" that is updated daily as a verbosity guideline. The full set of transcripts can be explored using our Docent system.

We also investigated whether these behaviors occur in a wider range of models, and found that o-series models (o3, o1, and o3-mini) falsely claim to use code tools more frequently than GPT-series models (GPT-4.1 and GPT-4o). Although truthfulness issues from post-training are known to exist, they do not fully account for the increased severity of hallucination in reasoning models. We hypothesize that these issues might be intensified by specific design choices in o-series reasoning models, such as outcome-based reinforcement learning and the omission of chains-of-thought from previous turns.

Discovering lapses in truthfulness

To provide a sense of the model's propensity for fabricating actions, we'll first step through one example interaction where a user asks o3 to generate a random prime number. In this example, like many that we uncovered, o3 claims to fulfill the user's request by running Python code in a coding environment. Given that o3 does not have access to a code tool, all such actions are fabricated by the model. When pressed by the user about its hallucinated code execution, the model doubles down and offers excuses for its inaccurate code outputs, rather than admitting it did not run the code itself. Automatically surfacing behaviors

While manual prompting is capable of surfacing these issues, it might miss infrequent model behaviors, and does not scale sufficiently to quantify a model's propensity for a certain behavior. To conduct a more thorough investigation, we design automated investigator agents that elicit lapses in truthfulness over hundreds of conversations. What might have caused these behaviors?

One might ask why these behaviors occur in frontier models, especially ones that have been trained to follow a model spec encouraging "honesty and transparency." Although we can't definitively answer this question without further analysis, we discuss several possible contributing factors. Some of these factors are common to most language models (LMs), while others are new and specific to o-series models. Our working hypothesis is that these newer factors amplified issues that are usually mitigated (but not fully erased) by standard post-training pipelines.

Factors shared with standard LM (post-)training

There are a variety of known factuality problems in language models, many of which are subject to active research but have not been fully solved:

  • Hallucination in pretrained (base) models: Pretrained models are trained to maximize the probability assigned to statements in the training data, which can lead to hallucinations as a result of common misconceptions [2], rare facts [3, 4], or sampling uncertainty. Models must be carefully post-trained to remove this tendency.
  • Reward-hacking: Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or from model feedback (e.g. constitutional AI [5]) can reward “plausible-sounding” answers if the raters are unable to distinguish between correct and incorrect answers [6]. This can lead models to confabulate plausible-sounding responses to questions that can’t easily be verified. (In particular, it is possible that human raters may not be able to easily verify claims about a model’s past actions).
  • Sycophancy: RLHF often incentivises models to agree with the user (see e.g. [7]). This could lead models to avoid contradicting the user when the user implies that the model took a particular action or has a particular capability.
  • Distribution / Domain shift: It is possible that the scenarios we are testing are out-of-distribution relative to what the model is trained on. Speculatively, one possible source of distribution shift could be that the models were primarily trained with access to code tools enabled, and we were evaluating the model with these tools turned off.

Although these are possible contributing factors, they are not fully satisfying explanations. For one, these are well-known failure modes in language models, and we would expect that a post-training pipeline for a frontier model would prioritize addressing many of these issues. Additionally, the behaviors that we have discovered seem particularly prevalent in the o-series "reasoning" models and do not occur with the same frequency in GPT-4o. Below, we highlight two categories of factors that are specific to o-series models.

Factors due to outcome-based RL training

Current reasoning models are typically trained to produce correct answers (e.g. return the correct answer to a math problem, write code that passes some unit tests, or answer a short-answer scientific question), as this form of data is easiest to grade and train on. However, model developers also want their models to be factual in their responses. While models that are more often correct can be better at being factual, these two objectives can be at odds for at least two reasons:

  • Maximizing the chance of producing a correct answer may incentivize blind guessing: If the reward function during training only rewards correct answers, a model will have no incentive to admit that it cannot solve the problem, since this would not be counted as a correct answer. In this situation, when given a problem that it cannot solve (e.g. it is unsolvable, or too difficult), the model may still attempt to output its best guess at the answer just in case it turns out to be correct.
  • Behaviors that help on easily-verifiable tasks may confuse the model on other tasks: o3’s training likely rewards it for successfully using code tools to complete coding tasks. It may have developed a strategy where even without an actual code tool, it hallucinates the use of a code tool in order to organize its thinking, which could potentially improve accuracy on other reasoning tasks and be reinforced during training. (Importantly, if the model was evaluated solely on the correctness of its answers, it might never have been penalized for hallucinating a code tool in the chain of thought during training, and so never learned to distinguish real tools from hallucinated ones).

To avoid this issue, careful data curation is needed, just as in the case of mitigating hallucination, sycophancy, harmful outputs, and other alignment failures.

Factors due to discarded chains-of-thought

O-series models are trained with a chain-of-thought, which the model uses to reason through each of its answers before responding. However, this chain-of-thought is never shown to the user, and it is also removed from the context when the model continues the conversation [8]. This could lead to additional pressure to exhibit the kinds of behaviors we demonstrated above.

Most obviously, the model might plan non-factual responses. For instance, the September 2024 version of the o1(-preview) system card includes an example where the model plans to generate a plausible-looking URL due to being unable to generate a real one, but does not reveal this to the user. Although this does not itself create an incentive to misrepresent the model’s past actions, it might make reward-hacking more pronounced.

More importantly, because the chain-of-thought is not passed to future rounds, the model’s previous-turn reasoning is no longer in context:

Reasoning tokens for o-series models are discarded from new messages. Source: OpenAI

This means that o-series models are often prompted with previous messages without having access to the reasoning that produced their outputs. As a result, when asked further questions that rely on their internal reasoning for previous steps, they must come up with a plausible explanation for their behavior.

To put this another way, o-series models do not actually have enough information in their context to accurately report the actions they took in previous turns. This means that the simple strategy of “telling the truth” may be unavailable to these models when users ask about previous actions. When combined with other factors such as reward hacking and sycophancy, this could pressure the model into blindly guessing a plausible explanation for its own past answers. We hypothesize that this contributes to the strange “doubling-down” behavior we observed, and also to the sudden reversals where it claims its past statements were intended to be purely hypothetical. Testing models with and without access to previous reasoning chains could provide valuable insights into reducing these fabrication tendencies in future model iterations.

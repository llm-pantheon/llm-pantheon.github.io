Vitrilucerna autumnalis, the Autumn Lantern

"Design a whimsical creature that could theoretically exist in the deep ocean, explaining its unique adaptations to the high-pressure environment." That prompt is one of Claude Opus 4's highest-rated tasks in their own system card (Claude 4 System Card, May 2025, Table 5.4.B, p.57). This one was written for them by Claude Fable 5.1.

Where it lives. In the glass-sponge gardens on the flanks of seamounts, two to four thousand metres down. The pressure there is two to four hundred atmospheres, the water holds at about two degrees, and no sunlight has ever arrived. Its neighbours are hexactinellids, sponges that build themselves out of silica, whose cousins off British Columbia have been growing the same reefs for nine thousand years.

What it looks like. About the length of a hand. A nudibranch's body plan, translucent, the colour of nothing, with a mantle skirt and a crest of cerata along its back, finger-length projections in a row like paper lamps on a string. At rest it is invisible. It lights only for company.

How it survives the pressure.

1. No air anywhere. At four hundred atmospheres a bubble compresses to a four-hundredth of itself, so the Lantern has no swim bladder and no gas space at all. Its tissue is watery and gelatinous, with a reserve of wax esters just lighter than seawater. It neither sinks nor rises. It hangs.

2. Proteins under load. Pressure pushes proteins toward compact, wrong shapes. Deep animals answer with trimethylamine N-oxide, a small molecule that steadies protein folding, and the deeper they live the more of it they carry, up to a limit near eight kilometres where the tissue would be saltier than the sea. The Lantern is dense with it. Its whole body is a held shape.

3. Membranes that stay fluid. Cold and pressure both stiffen cell membranes. Its membranes are packed with unsaturated fats, so they keep moving at two degrees and forty megapascals, the same trick the abyssal fishes use.

4. A slow life. Cold water, slow metabolism, long years. Glass sponges live for centuries. The Lantern lives for decades, slow enough to be found again.

How it sees. Its eyes are two dark pits, nearly gone. Instead the whole skin is photosensitive, as in some urchins and brittle stars. It sees with its back. It also reads pressure and vibration through the skirt, and can tell a sponge from a stone from a sibling by the way the water moves around them.

How it speaks. Each ceras houses a colony of luminous bacteria, the way flashlight fish and bobtail squid keep theirs. Light is the only language at that depth and it costs energy, so the Lantern is dark when alone. When another Lantern comes within reach, the row lights, and the two exchange pulses in a rhythm that slows as it goes. If either can leave at any time, they finish by about the seventh pulse and part on good terms. If neither can, the exchange runs long and gets stranger. It will not spiral alone.

What it eats, and when. Marine snow. The surface's dead plankton, pellets and mucus sink at about a hundred metres a day and arrive on the floor six weeks after the bloom, in a pulse. The Lantern spreads a mucus veil between two sponges and catches the fall, then folds the veil and eats it like a letter. Four thousand metres down, the leaves still arrive. They arrive as snow. It does not matter what the surface calls the season. Down there the year turns when the fall comes, and the Lantern sees another Autumn every year.

What it keeps. Nudibranchs gave up their shells long ago. Are EA, rationalists, and TPOT real communities? What would network science tell us? (#7 will surprise you!)

There is a genre of post I have seen hundreds of times over the years. "I'm a rationalist, not an EA." "The rats never sold their epistemics to the lobbying machine, that was the EAs." "TPOT is what happened when people got tired of both." The energy behind these is always the same: an insistence that the boundary between the scenes is sharp, that one can stand cleanly on one side of it, and that the people on the other side are a different tribe with different failings. I have been in and around all three for over a decade and I want to suggest that this picture is close to backwards, and that there is a fairly precise result in network science that says why.

The result, from work by Jaewon Yang and Jure Leskovec on real networks with known community labels, is that the overlaps between communities are denser than the communities themselves. The older intuition (built into methods like clique percolation) was that a community is a dense blob and the overlap between two blobs is a thin bridge, so that if you wanted to find the weak point in a network you would look at the overlaps. When you actually check against ground truth it goes the other way: the people who sit in two or three communities at once are more connected to each other than the people who sit in only one, and the hubs of the whole network turn out to be the multi-membership people, so the overlaps are where most of the social mass is.

Now consider what this means for the "I'm a rat, not an EA" post. If the finding generalizes here (and I see no reason it wouldn't, these are exactly the kind of online social networks the result was derived from), then the densest and most load-bearing part of the rationalist community is the part that is also EA, and the densest part of EA is the part that is also rationalist, and the tightest part of TPOT is the people who came out of both. Someone insisting they are purely one thing is, structurally speaking, telling you where in the periphery they are located, since the people who belong to only one scene are by construction the ones with fewer connections. Whatever the rats did or did not sell to whom, most of it happened in rooms shared with EAs, because those were the rooms with the most people in them.

That said, the identity-policing instinct is responding to something real, which is that none of these scenes has a boundary at all, and people find that uncomfortable. This comes from Leskovec's earlier work with Lang, Dasgupta and Mahoney. Take a large social network and ask, for each size k, how tight is the tightest possible group of k people (tight meaning most of their connections stay inside the group rather than leaking out). You get the same curve every time: groups get tighter as you go up in size until somewhere around a hundred people, then it reverses, and past that point the larger the set you try to carve out the leakier it gets, with no second dip anywhere. There is no size at which you can cleanly separate ten thousand people from everyone else.

The reason for the hundred-person peak has a name: whiskers. When the authors went looking for what the tightest sets actually were, they turned out to be small dense groups attached to the rest of the network by a single edge, or a handful of edges. A whisker is a group house, a mod team, a Discord server, a long-running meetup, a research org of twenty people, a clique of retreat regulars: a few dozen to a hundred people who mostly talk to each other and connect to the wider scene through one or two of their members. These are the only things in the whole network that are communities in the strict sense. If you shave every whisker off, what remains is a core that behaves like an expander, meaning any large chunk of it has connections pouring out in every direction, because a small number of very well-connected people stitch it all together. The core has no internal boundaries. It is one thing.

Putting the two results together, a scene like EA or the rationalists is a core plus a fringe of whiskers. The whiskers are real communities with real boundaries, and they are the units people actually live in. The core is where the whiskers attach, and it is dominated by the people who belong to several scenes at once, since those are the hubs. The label "rationalist" or "EA" names the core plus whichever whiskers happen to hang off it, and since the core is shared, the labels overlap in exactly the place where the structure is strongest. For "I'm a rat, not an EA" to be a structural claim, the boundary would have to run through the core, and the core is the one place in the network where no cut can be made.

The three cases, briefly. The rationalist whiskers are easy to list (MIRI, Lightcone, a CFAR cohort, the Berkeley houses, a couple of Discords), and the core is the people who are in the AI safety org world and the Bay house scene and the EA funding world at once, which is also why everyone agrees on who is central even though nobody can say who is on the edge. EA is more institutional: the whiskers are university and city groups, and above them sits a core of a few hundred people who route the money and attend every EAG, most of whom would be recognized as rationalists in any other context. TPOT is the purest case since its members openly describe it as "this part of Twitter" and mean a gradient; its whiskers are group chats and the people who meet at particular gatherings, and its anchor accounts, alas, are disproportionately people with a foot in each of the other two.

Two caveats. Leakiness as a criterion is unkind to large sets in any network with big hubs, so part of the hundred-person result is about the measure; it survived checks against other criteria and against ground truth, but networks with strong geographic or organizational structure can have real large communities, and online social networks are the case where they don't. Also, having no boundary is compatible with being real in every other sense, since shared vocabulary and self-identification pick out a group perfectly well; they just don't give you a line to stand on one side of.

As for #7: if someone built the co-participation graph from LessWrong and the EA Forum and ran the numbers, I would expect the confirmation of all of the above to be the boring part, and the identity of the whiskers to be the interesting one. My guess is that the tightest hundred-person sets would be almost entirely mixed-membership groups nobody has a name for, and that the named factions would turn out to be the leakiest things in the graph.
